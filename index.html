Silicon to Signal

Eighty years ago a computer filled a room and weighed thirty tons. Today a request crosses an ocean in a tenth of a second. This is the story of how we got from one to the other — and what actually happens when you press ENTER.

CHAPTER 01 · 1943 – 1955

The Room-Sized Mind

ENIAC computed with 17,468 vacuum tubes — fragile glass valves that glowed like small suns and failed, on average, every two days. Programming it meant rewiring it. Yet it could do in thirty seconds what took a human mathematician twenty hours.

CHAPTER 02 · 1947 – 1965

The Crystal That Changed Everything

In December 1947 at Bell Labs, a sliver of germanium did the job of a vacuum tube with no heat, no glass, no warm-up. The transistor was a switch made of nothing but matter and physics — small enough to vanish, reliable enough to forget. Everything after this is just the same trick, repeated trillions of times.

CHAPTER 03 · 1971 – PRESENT

A Computer on a Fingertip

The Intel 4004 put an entire CPU — 2,300 transistors — on one chip the size of a fingernail. Moore's Law became a metronome: transistor counts doubling roughly every two years, for fifty years. A modern flagship chip carries 20+ billion transistors, each smaller than a virus.

CHIPINTEL 4004 · 1971
TRANSISTORS2,300 → 2×10¹⁰
PROCESS10 µm → 2 nm

CHAPTER 04 · 1977 – 1995

The Machine Comes Home

Apple II. Commodore 64. IBM PC. Macintosh. The computer left the laboratory and landed on the kitchen table — and with the graphical interface, you no longer had to speak its language. It learned to speak yours. By 1995, a hundred million homes had one. They were all still alone.

CHAPTER 05 · 1969 – 1995

The Wires Wake Up

October 29, 1969: a UCLA computer sends LO to Stanford — the system crashes before the “GIN” of “LOGIN” arrives. From that stumble: ARPANET, then TCP/IP, then in 1989 Tim Berners-Lee proposes the World Wide Web. The machines stopped being alone.

INSIDE THE INTERNET · STEP 1 · ~0–30 ms

The Phonebook of the Planet

Computers don't know names — they know numbers. Before anything moves, your machine asks the Domain Name System: resolver → root server → .com TLD → authoritative nameserver. Four questions later, “example.com” becomes 93.184.215.14.

PATHROOT → TLD → AUTH

INSIDE THE INTERNET · STEP 2 · ~30–60 ms

The Three-Way Handshake

Now your machine and the server agree to talk — a formal bow before the conversation: SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK. Then TLS wraps the channel in encryption. Your request is shredded into packets — independent envelopes, each free to find its own route and reassemble at the destination.

HANDSHAKESYN / SYN-ACK / ACK
CRYPTOTLS 1.3
MTU~1,500 BYTES
ORDERANY → REASSEMBLED

INSIDE THE INTERNET · STEP 3 · ~60–120 ms

Light Under the Ocean

Your packets hop router to router — each one reading the destination and choosing the next leg in microseconds. Between continents they become pulses of laser light inside submarine fiber cables the width of a garden hose, lying on the ocean floor. Around 500 cables carry ~99% of intercontinental traffic.

INSIDE THE INTERNET · STEP 4 · ~120–180 ms

The Building That Answers

Your request arrives at a windowless building humming at 45 dB — a data center. Load balancers pick a healthy server; the app queries a database; a response is assembled, compressed, encrypted, and fired back down the same wire. Football fields of racks, cooled like a glacier, lit like a starship.

The Weather of Intelligence

Every era of this story is still running, stacked — tubes became transistors became chips became networks became a substrate for intelligence itself.
